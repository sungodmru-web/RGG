2.2.4 Informal Systems 
Another unintended consequence of prohibition is the expansion and entrenchment of 
informal economic systems. In many LMIC, cannabis production and trade are 
embedded in local livelihood strategies. Prohibition does not eliminate these activities; 
instead, it shifts them into informal or illicit domains where they are less visible but 
often more resilient (UNODC, 2023). This can create parallel systems that operate 
outside state oversight, complicating governance and limiting opportunities for formal 
economic participation. 
The interaction between prohibition and development dynamics is consequential. In 
countries where economic opportunities are limited, cannabis cultivation may represent 
one of the few viable income sources. Strict enforcement can have unintended effects 
on livelihoods, potentially exacerbating poverty or encouraging further informality. The 
absence of regulation prevents these activities from contributing to formal economic 
systems through taxation, value addition, or employment generation (WB, 2017). 
In several African countries, cannabis cultivation has historically provided income for 
smallholder farmers in marginal areas with limited agricultural alternatives. Enforcement 
measures that target these producers without providing viable alternatives can 
undermine rural livelihoods and fuel discontent or resistance. This reveals the need for 
development-sensitive approaches that address the underlying economic drivers of 
cannabis production. 
Prohibition creates barriers to policy innovation. Legal constraints and institutional 
inertia may limit the ability of governments to experiment with alternative approaches, 
even where existing systems are not delivering desired outcomes. This can result in 
policy stagnation, in which reforms are delayed despite evidence of their limitations 
(Bewley-Taylor et al., 2016). The international treaty, while increasingly flexible in 
interpretation, continues to exert a constraining influence on national policy choices. 
2.2.5 LMIC Contexts 
For LMIC, mostly in the African region, these limitations are often magnified by 
structural constraints. Limited institutional capacity, high levels of informality, and 
competing development priorities shape how prohibition functions in practice. The 
result is a hybrid system in which formal prohibition coexists with informal tolerance 
or selective enforcement (WB, 2020). 
In SIDS, geographic factors compound these challenges. The difficulty of monitoring 
extensive coastlines and maritime borders facilitates illicit trade, while limited 
enforcement resources constrain the ability to interdict supply. Meanwhile, tourism adds 
another layer of complexity. Governments must balance preserving a reputation for 
safety with enforcing prohibition against visitors from jurisdictions with more 
permissive cannabis policies. These dynamics show that prohibition is not simply a legal 
framework but a complex system with unintended consequences. Recognising these 
consequences is essential to more effective policy design. The next section explores the 
transition from suppression to governance.
