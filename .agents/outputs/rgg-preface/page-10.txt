Guidance for Policymakers  
Cannabis policy is a test of institutional capability, policy coherence, and social contract, 
distinctly in LMIC and SIDS. Above all, this book underpins that developmental 
outcomes are determined by governance design, not market size, climate, or 
comparative advantage. 
Sequence reform of institutional readiness, not market ambition. Prioritise first things 
first: clear mandates, basic licensing, and public literacy before commercial upscale.  
The temptation to move fast is understandable; the costs of moving faster than capacity 
allows are well documented in this book. 
Use regional cooperation as a strategic platform. Shared laboratories, harmonised 
standards, pooled training, and mutual recognition of certification materially reduce 
compliance costs for smaller economies. For SIDS in particular, regional cooperation 
transforms structural smallness into coordinated capability that no single island could 
achieve alone. 
Treat equity and inclusion as core design principles, not remedial afterthoughts. 
Reforms that fail to address the disproportionate harms of prohibition erode public 
trust and sustain illicit markets. Inclusion is not a political concession; it is a foundational 
condition of legitimacy and long-term market stability. 
Invest in adaptive governance systems. We manage what we can measure. Monitoring, 
evaluation, and learning loops are essential infrastructure — not bureaucratic overhead 
— for recalibrating policy in response to real-world outcomes. 
Apply the Governance Transition Model as a diagnostic tool. Assess national readiness 
across the seven layers, identify critical gaps, and sequence investments to strengthen 
weak points before scaling. The model is not a prescription — it is a mirror, designed 
to surface the gaps that political momentum tends to obscure. 
Cannabis is a microcosm of 21st-century governance challenges. The lessons learned 
here — about sequencing reform under institutional constraints, retaining value in 
global markets, repairing historical harm, operationalising sustainability principles and 
building adaptive systems under uncertainty — will prove directly transferable to climate 
adaptation, digital transformation, and the other complex transitions that define this era 
of governance. 
The window for deliberate design is open. What this book ultimately argues is that 
countries which invest in the seven governance layers now — before markets 
consolidate, before regulatory capture sets in, before the political moment passes — will 
not merely regulate cannabis better. They will have built governance infrastructure 
capable of navigating whatever complex transition comes next. That is the deeper prize. 
It will not remain available indefinitely.
