2.2.2 Illicit Market Risks 
The existence of illicit markets has important implications for public health and safety. 
Products circulating in unregulated systems are not subject to quality standards, 
increasing risks related to contamination, potency variability, and mislabelling. In the 
absence of testing requirements, cannabis may contain pesticides, heavy metals, 
microbial contaminants, or adulterants that pose health risks to consumers. Potency 
variability is another concern. Illicit markets provide limited information about 
cannabinoid content, making it difficult for consumers to moderate dosage or anticipate 
effects. The concern is heightened by the widespread availability of high-potency 
cannabis products on the market. 
Without regulatory oversight, consumers lack the information needed to make informed 
decisions about use. The absence of formal oversight also limits the ability of authorities 
to implement harm reduction measures or to provide accurate information to 
consumers. Public health messaging about cannabis risks and safer use practices is more 
difficult to deliver when the product is entirely illicit. Furthermore, the stigma associated 
with illegality may discourage individuals from seeking medical advice or disclosing use 
to healthcare providers (WHO, 2019). 
2.2.3 Social Consequences 
Prohibition has also generated significant social consequences. Criminalisation can lead 
to arrest, prosecution, and incarceration, with long-term implications for individuals' 
livelihoods and social inclusion. Criminal records related to cannabis offences can affect 
employment prospects, housing access, educational opportunities, and civic 
participation. These collateral consequences often extend far beyond the immediate 
penalty, creating lasting barriers to social and economic integration. In many LMIC, 
enforcement outcomes are uneven. They often disproportionately affect small-scale 
producers and low-income populations. (Alexander, 2010; GCDP, 2018).  
These patterns raise concerns about equity and justice where enforcement is selective 
or inconsistent. Racial and ethnic disparities in cannabis-related arrests and prosecutions 
have been documented in multiple jurisdictions, including the United States, the United 
Kingdom, and Canada. From an institutional perspective, prohibition places significant 
demands on public resources. Law enforcement, judicial systems, and correctional 
institutions require sustained funding to maintain control efforts.  
In resource-constrained environments, these expenditures must be balanced against 
other priorities, including health, education, and infrastructure. Where outcomes 
diverge from policy objectives, cost-effectiveness becomes a pressing consideration 
(Miron & Waldock, 2010). The opportunity costs of prohibition are high. Redirecting 
resources from cannabis enforcement to prevention, treatment, and public health could 
generate greater social benefits, particularly in fiscally constrained LMIC.
