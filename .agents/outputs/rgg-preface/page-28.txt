2.1.2 Enforcement Realities 
Enforcement constitutes the operational core of prohibition. Law enforcement agencies 
are tasked with detecting and suppressing illicit activities across the cannabis value 
chain—from cultivation and trafficking to retail distribution and consumption. This 
involves a range of tools, including surveillance, interdiction, arrest, and prosecution. 
The effectiveness of these measures depends heavily on institutional capacity, resource 
allocation, and coordination across agencies. 
However, prohibition is not uniformly enforced. In practice, enforcement outcomes 
vary significantly across and within countries. Resource constraints, geographic factors, 
and political priorities shape how laws are applied. In many contexts, enforcement is 
selective, focusing on visible or accessible segments of the market rather than on more 
complex or organised networks. This uneven application can create inconsistencies and 
undermine the perceived legitimacy of the system. 
This dynamic is more pronounced in LMIC, where the combination of constrained 
enforcement capacity and deeply rooted informal economies creates conditions in 
which illicit markets do not merely persist — they adapt, consolidate, and at times 
outpace the legal frameworks presumably designed to displace them. 
2.1.3 Interaction with Informal Systems 
Another key feature of prohibition is its interaction with informal economies. In many 
LMIC settings, cannabis production and distribution are deeply rooted in informal 
livelihood systems. Prohibition does not eliminate these activities; rather, it pushes them 
outside formal regulatory frameworks. As a result, significant portions of the cannabis 
economy operate without oversight, taxation, or quality control. This creates challenges 
for both governance and development, as informal systems can be resilient but difficult 
to regulate. 
The relationship between prohibition and informality is complex. In some contexts, 
prohibition itself creates the conditions for informal markets to thrive by restricting 
legal alternatives. Producers and distributors who might otherwise operate within 
formal systems are forced into informality, where they face enforcement risks but also 
benefit from the absence of regulatory costs. This dynamic can create a self-reinforcing 
cycle in which prohibition sustains the very activities it seeks to eliminate. 
For many small-scale cultivators worldwide, cannabis production represents a rational 
economic response to limited alternatives. In these contexts, enforcement efforts that 
target production without addressing underlying livelihood constraints may simply 
displace activity rather than eliminate it. Understanding this context is essential for 
designing effective policy responses that move beyond suppression towards sustainable 
alternatives.
