2.6.2 Africa: Livelihoods and Structural Transformation 
In many African countries, where smallholder agriculture remains central to livelihoods, 
cannabis policy intersects directly with rural development and land-use strategies. 
Cannabis cultivation already exists in informal yet economically significant systems, 
which means that policy pathways must address the transition from informality to 
regulated participation. 
This requires licensing systems that are accessible to small-scale producers. Traditional 
models developed in advanced economies often involve high costs, complex 
procedures, and stringent facility requirements beyond the reach of most smallholders. 
Alternative approaches—such as tiered licensing, cooperative models, and phased 
compliance pathways—can support broader participation. 
Cooperative structures play a strategic role in enabling smallholders to pool production, 
share compliance costs, and strengthen their bargaining power within value chains. In 
predominantly small-scale agricultural economies, they may offer a more inclusive 
organisational model than individual commercial licensing. 
Technical assistance, delivered through effective extension services, is essential to 
translate cultivation knowledge into regulatory compliance. While many smallholders 
possess extensive cultivation experience, they often need support to meet Good 
Agricultural Practices (GAP), product testing, and documentation requirements. 
Strengthening extension systems can improve compliance, reduce barriers to market 
entry, and promote more inclusive participation in regulated markets. 
African countries must also make strategic decisions about market orientation. Export-
oriented models may provide access to high-value medical cannabis markets, but they 
require strict compliance with international standards and expose producers to external 
market volatility. GMP certification for pharmaceutical-grade exports requires 
significant investment and may be beyond the reach of many domestic operators 
without external support. 
Domestic and regional markets may offer more stable, accessible, and inclusive 
opportunities while institutional capacity is still evolving. They can stimulate local value 
addition, create employment, and strengthen supply chains before countries compete in 
highly regulated international markets. Regional integration initiatives such as the 
African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) could further support cross-border 
cannabis trade, expand market size, encourage regulatory harmonisation, and reduce 
dependence on volatile global demand. Balancing domestic, regional, and export 
markets requires careful assessment of economic opportunities, institutional capacity, 
and implementation feasibility.
