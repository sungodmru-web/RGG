By reframing cannabis policy as a governance question rather than a legal one, the 
chapter provides a foundation for more coherent and context-sensitive policy design. It 
highlights the importance of institutional capacity, policy sequencing, and adaptive 
systems in shaping outcomes. It sets the stage for the more detailed examination of

This transition should be considered as part of a broader systemic shift. Legal reform 
alone does not redefine cannabis policy; it interacts with institutional capacity, social 
legitimacy, knowledge systems, and economic structures. The diversity of emerging 
regulatory approaches reflects deeper dynamics that shape how policies are designed, 
implemented, and experienced. Understanding cannabis governance requires looking 
beyond legal classifications to the systems that enable or constrain reform. 
2.1 The Architecture of Prohibition 
The prohibition of cannabis is not simply a legal choice; it is a structured policy system 
with defined institutional, legal, and operational components. Understanding its 
architecture is essential to assessing both its resilience and its limitations. Rather than a 
static rule, prohibition is a multi-layered framework of international norms, national 
laws, and enforcement systems. 
2.1.1 Institutional Foundations 
At the international level, prohibition has been anchored in a series of drug control 
conventions that constituted a coordinated global approach. The 1961 Single 
Convention on Narcotic Drugs5, as amended by the 1972 Protocol, created a normative 
framework within which cannabis was classified as a controlled substance subject to 
strict limitations on production, distribution, and use. The 1971 Convention on 
Psychotropic Substances and the 1988 Convention against Illicit Traffic in Narcotic 
Drugs and Psychotropic Substances further reinforced this framework, establishing 
obligations for criminalisation and international cooperation. While countries retain 
sovereignty in implementation, these conventions have historically shaped national 
policy choices and constrained the range of permissible approaches. 
National legal systems translate these international commitments into domestic law. 
Typically, this has involved the criminalisation of cultivation, possession, and 
distribution, supported by penal sanctions and enforcement procedures. Over time, 
these legal frameworks have become embedded within broader criminal justice systems, 
influencing policing practices, judicial processes, and correctional institutions. In many 
countries, prohibition is not an isolated policy, but part of a wider legal and institutional 
ecosystem. The entrenchment of prohibition within national legal systems has created 
pronounced path dependency. Once established, these frameworks generate 
institutional inertia, making reform difficult even when their rationale is questioned.
