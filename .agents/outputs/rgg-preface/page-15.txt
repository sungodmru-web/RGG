Cannabis policy has become a test case for broader governance questions—how states 
manage emerging industries, regulate complex markets, and align policy objectives with 
development priorities. 
1.2 Post-prohibition as a Policy Paradigm 
For much of the twentieth century, prohibition provided the dominant framework for 
cannabis control. Anchored in national legislation and reinforced by international 
conventions, it established a clear legal position: cannabis production, distribution, and 
use were to be suppressed through criminalisation and enforcement. This paradigm 
shaped institutional structures, policy priorities, and public narratives for decades. 
1.2.1 The Limits of Prohibition 
Prohibition offered certain advantages: a uniform legal standard, a strong normative 
stance against drug use, and coordinated international action. However, its limitations 
have become increasingly apparent: it has not eliminated cannabis demand or supply. 
Instead, it has contributed to the persistence of informal and illicit markets operating 
outside regulatory oversight, undermining public health objectives and weakening the 
effectiveness of enforcement (UNODC, 2023). 
Prohibition has also generated high social and economic costs. Enforcement 
expenditure has been high, and criminalisation has disproportionately affected certain 
populations, raising equity and justice concerns. In many contexts, small-scale or 
subsistence actors have borne the brunt of enforcement while larger networks have 
proven more difficult to dismantle (GCDP, 2018). 
At the same time, broader shifts in scientific understanding and public attitudes have 
challenged the assumptions underpinning prohibition. Research highlights both the 
risks and potential therapeutic applications of cannabis, complicating its classification 
as a substance addressed solely by criminal law. Public opinion in many countries 
increasingly favours approaches that prioritise public health, harm reduction, and 
regulation over punitive measures (WHO, 2019). 
1.2.2 Impact on Governance 
These developments have prompted a gradual but considerable rethinking of cannabis 
policy. Governments are exploring alternatives that manage cannabis-related activities 
more effectively rather than attempting elimination. This shift recognises that 
prohibition, while historically dominant, is not the only available framework. The 
transition away from prohibition does not imply deregulation. Emerging approaches 
rely on structured regulatory systems with licensing, standards and oversight. The aim 
is to improve public health, increase transparency, and strengthen market management. 
For LMIC, prohibition's limitations are acutely pertinent. Enforcement capacity is often 
constrained, and informal economies play a critical role in livelihoods. Strict prohibition 
can be both difficult to enforce and economically disruptive. A rapid transition to fully 
liberalised markets may exceed institutional capacity and create new risks. The challenge 
is to rethink the policy paradigm, not simply replace prohibition.
