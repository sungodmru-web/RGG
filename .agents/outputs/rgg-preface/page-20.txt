1.5.2 Institutional Readiness 
Institutional readiness determines policy effectiveness. The ability to implement and 
enforce cannabis policy depends on the capacity to manage licensing systems, monitor 
compliance, uphold product standards, and coordinate across sectors. Legal reform 
without institutional capability risks creating regulatory gaps that informal or illicit 
markets can exploit. In many LMIC, where administrative capacity remains weak, 
institutional readiness is often the principal constraint on policy ambition (WB, 2017). 
Institutional readiness is not static. It can be strengthened over time through targeted 
investments in human resources, regulatory systems, digital infrastructure, and 
organisational learning. This requires realistic sequencing, with policies aligned to 
existing capabilities and more complex regulatory functions introduced progressively as 
capacity develops. Continuous training, knowledge exchange, and adaptive learning also 
enable institutions to respond to emerging evidence and evolving market conditions. 
Where institutional readiness is weak, even the most sophisticated legal framework 
remains largely aspirational. Where it is deliberately strengthened, reform becomes a 
platform for credible, effective, and durable governance (Acemoglu & Robinson, 2012). 
1.5.3 Adaptive Governance 
Adaptive governance recognises that cannabis policy operates in a dynamic 
environment. Scientific knowledge evolves, markets diversify, and social attitudes shift. 
As a result, static regulatory frameworks are unlikely to remain effective. Governance 
systems should therefore be designed to learn, adapt, and respond to new evidence 
through continuous monitoring, evaluation, and periodic policy review (Ostrom, 1990). 
Adaptive governance also requires managing uncertainty. Policymakers often operate 
with incomplete or contested evidence on public health, market dynamics, and social 
impacts. Rather than waiting for certainty, they should adopt an iterative approach: 
implement, monitor, evaluate, and adjust as evidence and experience accumulate. This 
enables policies to remain effective while responding to emerging risks and 
opportunities. 
These three principles are closely interconnected. Differentiation informs context-
specific policy design, institutional readiness determines the pace and scope of 
implementation, and adaptive governance ensures that policies evolve as conditions 
change. Together, they provide a coherent framework for navigating the complexities 
of cannabis reform. Rather than framing reform as a choice between prohibition and 
legalisation, this framework focuses on governance quality, institutional learning, and 
the capacity to balance multiple public policy objectives. This perspective is particularly 
relevant for LMIC, where reform must remain responsive to changing realities while 
supporting broader development priorities and institutional capacity.
