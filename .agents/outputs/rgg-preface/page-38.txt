2.4.5 Regulatory Trade-offs  
These dimensions illustrate that the choice of a regulatory model involves trade-offs. 
Increasing market participation may generate economic benefits but requires stronger 
regulatory oversight. Simplifying systems may improve feasibility but limit policy 
control. Enhancing inclusiveness may require additional support mechanisms and 
institutional innovation. For LMIC, this framework provides a practical tool for policy 
positioning. It allows governments to assess where they are on the continuum, where 
they aim to move, and what institutional adjustments are required to support that 
transition. The framework reinforces that movement along the continuum should be 
incremental and adaptive.  
Attempting to shift rapidly towards complex regulatory systems without adequate 
institutional capacity can create implementation gaps and unintended consequences. 
Conversely, gradual progression allows for learning, adjustment, and system 
strengthening. As such, the governance continuum is more than an analytical construct; 
it is a strategic instrument for guiding reform. It supports a shift from static policy 
choices to dynamic pathways that evolve in response to changing conditions and policy 
objectives. It also reveals that regulatory models cannot be assessed in isolation from 
the broader systems within which they operate. Similar legal frameworks can produce 
very different outcomes depending on institutional capacity, administrative coherence, 
and the degree of alignment between formal rules and social practices. 
Cannabis regulation is not simply a matter of legal design, but of governance quality. 
The effectiveness of any model depends on the interaction between evidence, 
institutions, and legitimacy—factors that shape implementation, compliance, and long-
term sustainability. This systemic perspective matters because policy outcomes are never 
shaped by legislation alone. Jurisdiction, capacity, history, and context determine what 
works — which is why adaptive, context-sensitive approaches consistently outperform 
imported models. 
2.5 From Theory to Practice 
Building on the governance continuum, this section examines the principal models 
through which cannabis is currently regulated in practice. These models are not mutually 
exclusive, and sometimes, countries combine elements from different approaches. They 
also help explain how policy objectives are translated into regulatory design. 
2.5.1 Decriminalisation-Based Models 
A first category is decriminalisation-based models. These approaches remove or reduce 
criminal penalties for possession and, in some cases, personal cultivation, while 
maintaining prohibition on commercial production and distribution. The primary 
objective is to reduce the social and economic costs of criminalising end-users. 
Portugal's 2001 decriminalisation of all drugs, including cannabis, is often cited as a 
prominent example. Under this framework, possession for personal use remains illegal 
but is treated as an administrative rather than a criminal offence, with cases referred to 
dissuasion commissions rather than criminal courts.
