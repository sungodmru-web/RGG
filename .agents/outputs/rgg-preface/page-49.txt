3.0.5 Structure of the Chapter 
This chapter focuses on key phases that have shaped the contemporary cannabis 
landscape: early domestication and civilizational uses (3.1), India and the Vedic–
Ayurveda system (3.2), diffusion across the Middle East and Africa (3.3), Mediterranean 
and classical systems (3.4), Europe and the hemp economy (3.5), Latin America and the 
Caribbean (3.6), North America from hemp to prohibition (3.7), the geopolitics of a 
divided global system (3.8), global value chains and economic positioning (3.9), the 
emergence of early international controls (3.10), and historical lessons for governance 
(3.11). Each phase is analysed in its historical context, besides its relevance for current 
and future governance. In doing so, the chapter advances a critical argument: 
contemporary cannabis governance is inseparable from its historical trajectories. The transition from 
prohibition to governance, examined in Chapter 2, can only be fully understood—and 
effectively implemented—when grounded in this historical perspective. 
3.1 Early Civilizational Uses 
Cannabis is among the earliest domesticated plants, with archaeological evidence tracing 
its origins to Central and East Asia more than 10,000 years ago (Clarke & Merlin, 2016). 
From the outset, it was not associated with a single economic or cultural function. 
Instead, it emerged as a highly versatile resource united in multiple domains of early 
human societies, including fibre production, nutrition, medicine, and ritual practice. 
3.1.1 Early Societal Integration 
In Neolithic China, hemp fibres were widely used for textiles, rope, and early forms of 
paper, contributing to both household economies and state formation processes. 
Cannabis seeds provided a valuable source of nutrition, rich in oils and proteins, while 
early medicinal texts documented their therapeutic applications for pain, inflammation, 
and digestive conditions. The Shennong Bencao Jing, one of the earliest Chinese 
pharmacopoeias dating to approximately 200 CE, described cannabis preparations for 
various ailments, reflecting systematic observation and accumulated medical knowledge. 
Similar patterns are observed across Central Asia, where archaeological findings in burial 
sites indicate the use of cannabis in ceremonial contexts. The discovery of resin-rich 
cannabis remains in the Tarim Basin, dating to approximately 2500 BCE, suggests both 
pharmacological and symbolic roles in funerary rituals (Russo, 2007). Archaeological 
evidence from the Pazyryk culture in Siberia suggests that cannabis formed part of ritual 
practices. Tent-like structures containing braziers and cannabis seeds indicate 
intentional inhalation. 
This multi-functionality distinguishes cannabis from many other early crops. Rather 
than being confined to a specific sector, it operated as a systemic resource embedded 
within broader socio-economic and cultural systems. Its spread across regions was more 
than a biological process.
