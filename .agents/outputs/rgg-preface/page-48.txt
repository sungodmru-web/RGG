3.0.2 Trade Routes and Cultural Diffusion 
The diffusion of cannabis across Eurasia, Africa, and later the Americas was closely 
linked to patterns of migration, trade, and empire. Along major exchange routes—
including the Silk Road, the Indian Ocean trade network, and trans-Saharan corridors—
the plant travelled together with knowledge systems governing its use. These systems 
were often locally adapted to reflect ecological conditions, cultural practices, and social 
norms. In this sense, cannabis was a biological commodity besides a carrier of 
accumulated knowledge. 
The Silk Road, stretching from China through Central Asia to the Middle East and 
Europe, facilitated the movement of goods as well as the transmission of medicinal and 
cultural practices associated with cannabis. Similarly, Indian Ocean trade networks 
connected the subcontinent with East Africa and the Arabian Peninsula, enabling the 
spread of cannabis cultivation and consumption practices across diverse ecological and 
cultural zones. Trans-Saharan caravan routes further extended this diffusion into West 
Africa, where cannabis was integrated into local livelihood systems and ritual practices. 
3.0.3 Historical Pluralism  
The transition from this pluralistic historical landscape to the rigid prohibition 
frameworks of the twentieth century represents a significant rupture. Rather than 
emerging from a gradual evolution of scientific understanding, prohibition was shaped 
by a combination of geopolitical interests, colonial governance practices, and shifting 
social narratives (Bewley-Taylor, 2012). These regulatory systems frequently displaced 
existing knowledge structures while overlooking the socio-economic functions cannabis 
continued to serve, most visibly in LMIC. 
Colonial administrations played a crucial role in reshaping cannabis governance. In 
many African and Asian territories under European control, cannabis was reframed 
from a locally regulated resource into a substance requiring external control. This 
reframing was often driven by concerns about labour discipline, social order, and 
perceived threats to colonial authority rather than by systematic assessment of 
cannabis's effects on health or society (Mills, 2016). 
3.0.4 Relevance for Contemporary Governance 
These historical dynamics have direct implications for contemporary governance. The 
persistence of informal markets, the uneven enforcement of laws, and the tensions 
between global regulatory frameworks and local realities can all be traced, at least in 
part, to this legacy. In many LMIC, cannabis remains entrenched in livelihood systems 
and cultural practices, creating a mismatch between formal legal frameworks and lived 
realities. The historical evolution of cannabis is not merely a record of past practices but 
a foundation for understanding contemporary governance challenges. The ways in 
which societies have cultivated, regulated, and interpreted cannabis over time continue 
to shape present-day policy choices, institutional arrangements, and public perceptions.
