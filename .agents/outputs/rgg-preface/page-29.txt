2.1.4 Economic Costs  
The economic dimension of prohibition is often overlooked — yet it is what sustains 
its functioning. Enforcement requires sustained public expenditure on policing, judicial 
processes, and incarceration. Notably, prohibition forecloses potential revenue streams 
associated with regulated markets, including taxation and licensing. These fiscal 
implications become increasingly relevant as governments reassess the cost-
effectiveness of existing approaches. Studies in various jurisdictions have attempted to 
quantify the costs of prohibition. These costs include direct expenditure on law 
enforcement, court proceedings, and correctional facilities. They also include indirect 
costs, such as lost productivity, forgone tax revenue, and the broader social 
consequences of criminalisation (Miron & Waldock, 2010).  
The cumulative fiscal impact is substantial, though estimates vary; a burden felt most 
acutely in countries with high rates of cannabis-related enforcement. Prohibition 
assumes that criminalisation deters use, that enforcement can disrupt supply, and that 
the benefits of control outweigh the associated social and economic costs. These 
assumptions have shaped policy for decades, yet empirical evidence is challenging their 
validity by showing persistent demand and adaptive supply systems (UNODC, 2023). 
2.1.5 Variations in Implementation 
Prohibition is not monolithic because countries vary in how they interpret and 
implement control measures. Some jurisdictions apply strict enforcement across all 
aspects of the cannabis market, while others adopt more tolerant approaches in practice, 
even where formal laws remain restrictive. These variations reflect differences in 
institutional capacity, political priorities, and societal attitudes. 
For example, several European countries maintain formal prohibition but have adopted 
policies of de facto tolerance for personal possession or small-scale cultivation. In the 
Netherlands, the coffee shop6 system allows the regulated sale of cannabis under specific 
conditions, despite cannabis remaining technically illegal under national law. Similarly, 
Spain permits cannabis social clubs, where members collectively cultivate and consume 
cannabis in a non-commercial setting. These examples show how countries navigate the 
space between legal prohibition and practical governance. 
For LMIC, the architecture of prohibition often interacts with institutional challenges. 
Limited enforcement capacity, competing policy priorities, and the prevalence of 
informal economic activity shape how prohibition functions in practice. The system 
combines formal legal restrictions with de facto tolerance and selective enforcement. 
Understanding prohibition as a system, rather than a legal rule, is essential to analysing 
its evolution.  It reveals why the regime has proven both durable and contested. Its 
institutional foundations sustain stability, while its systemic limitations drive reform. 
                                                   
6 The "coffee shop" model refers to the Dutch policy of tolerating the retail sale of small quantities of cannabis under regulated 
conditions, despite cannabis remaining formally prohibited under national law. This pragmatic approach separates the retail 
market ("front door") from the illicit supply chain ("back door"), illustrating how enforcement discretion can create de facto 
regulatory arrangements within a framework of de jure prohibition.
