Prologue – “Transforming Challenge into Opportunity” 
Across the developing world, cannabis is being legalised faster than it is being governed. 
Licences are issued before laboratories exist. Export frameworks are announced before 
certification systems are built. The gap between legislative ambition and institutional 
reality is not a minor detail—it is the defining challenge of this transition, and the reason 
this book was written. 
For nations across Africa, the Caribbean, and the Indian Ocean, cannabis offers 
pathways unimaginable a decade ago. It can revitalise declining rural economies, anchor 
green industrialisation through hemp, and enable Small Island Developing States to 
compete in high‑value medical and wellness markets. Countries rich in traditional 
knowledge—from Morocco's Rif Mountains to Jamaica's farming communities—can 
transform Indigenous wisdom into valued assets. Medical cannabis alone holds 
transformative potential: reducing dependence on expensive imports and legitimising 
traditional medicine through scientific validation. 
Yet realising these opportunities means confronting structural challenges. Regulatory 
frameworks remain designed for prohibition rather than governance. Laboratory 
infrastructure is inadequate. Banking services remain inaccessible. Without deliberate 
institutional strengthening, reform benefits risk capture by well‑capitalised outsiders, 
leaving local communities and legacy cultivators marginalised. These are not merely 
technical failures but distributional injustices. 
Justice is not a footnote. Communities that bore prohibition's heaviest costs—
smallholders criminalised for subsistence, Indigenous practitioners whose knowledge 
was appropriated, populations over‑policed while others operated freely—are owed 
more than belated inclusion. They are owed recognition, repair, and genuine 
participation in governing the systems now built in their name. Reforms that fail here 
reproduce the asymmetries they claim to leave behind. 
What these challenges consistently require is governance: the patient work of building 
institutions, aligning incentives, and designing policies that are honest about what states 
can deliver. That work is unglamorous. It rarely makes headlines. However, it is the 
work on which every other ambition depends. 
This book is for those doing that work: policymakers navigating reform with limited 
resources, development practitioners building capacity where it is scarce, and 
governance reformers who understand that durable change is built slowly, 
institutionally, and inclusively. Developing countries can be active architects of systems 
that serve their own people and advance their own futures. 
"Legalisation is the easy part. Readiness is the work."
