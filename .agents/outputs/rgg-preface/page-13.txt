Chapter 1: Cannabis in the 21st Century 
"The problem is not drugs; the problem is the conditions under which people use them."

Abstract 
Cannabis has emerged as a central policy issue of the twenty-first century, extending beyond its historical 
framing as a criminal justice or public health issue. It now intersects with economic transformation, 
environmental sustainability, and questions of equity and development. Across regions, countries are 
moving—at different speeds and with varying institutional capacities—away from prohibition towards 
new regulatory approaches. 
This chapter frames cannabis reform as a transition from prohibition-based control to governance-
oriented systems. It argues that legal change alone is insufficient and that outcomes depend primarily on 
institutional capacity, policy design, and implementation. The convergence of scientific advances, market 
expansion and evolving governance demands is reshaping how cannabis is understood, regulated and 
governed.  
Greater emphasis is laid on low- and middle-income countries (LMIC) 1and Small Island Developing 
States (SIDS) worldwide, where cannabis presents opportunities for diversification alongside risks of 
exclusion and external capture. The chapter introduces three guiding principles—differentiation, 
institutional readiness, and adaptive governance—which underpin the analysis throughout the book. It 
establishes cannabis reform as a complex, long-term policy process embedded within broader development 
and sustainability transitions. 
1.0 Introduction 
Cannabis policy is undergoing a profound and uneven transformation. Governments 
worldwide are re-evaluating prohibition-based approaches2 and experimenting with 
alternative regulatory models. What was once treated primarily as a criminal justice 
concern is increasingly addressed as a multidimensional policy challenge that 
encompasses health, economic development, and institutional governance (UNODC, 
2023; WHO, 2019). 
This shift reflects a broader reassessment of the effectiveness of prohibition. While 
prohibition established a clear legal framework and coordinated international control 
system, it has not eliminated cannabis production, distribution, or use. It has instead 
contributed to the persistence of informal markets, uneven enforcement outcomes, and 
high social costs, including criminalisation and exclusion. The question facing 
policymakers is no longer simply whether cannabis should remain illegal, but how it 
should be governed to balance competing objectives.
