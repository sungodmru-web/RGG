2.3.3 Institutional Pressures 
Public attitudes have shifted significantly. In many countries, support for punitive 
approaches to cannabis has declined, while acceptance of regulatory models has 
increased. This shift reflects broader changes in social norms and growing awareness of 
the limitations and unintended consequences of prohibition. Public opinion does not 
determine policy on its own, but it shapes the political space within which reforms 
become possible (Pew Research Center, 2021). 
Generational gap is an important factor in shifting attitudes. Younger cohorts, who have 
come of age in an era of more open debate about cannabis policy, tend to express greater 
support for legalisation or regulation than older generations. As these cohorts comprise 
a larger share of the electorate, political incentives for reform increase. 
Fiscal and institutional considerations add another layer of pressure. The costs 
associated with enforcing prohibition, including policing, judicial processes, and 
incarceration, are substantial. In resource-constrained settings, these expenditures are 
increasingly scrutinised for their effectiveness. (Miron & Waldock, 2010). The inability 
to capture revenue from illicit markets represents a lost fiscal opportunity, further 
incentivising reconsideration of existing approaches. 
A further driver is the growing recognition of governance limitations. As highlighted in 
earlier sections, prohibition has struggled to address complex and adaptive market 
dynamics. Illicit systems have proven resilient, and enforcement outcomes have often 
been uneven. These realities have prompted a shift towards approaches that prioritise 
regulation and management over suppression (GCDP, 2018). 
2.3.4 International Dynamics  
While global drug control conventions continue to provide a reference framework, their 
interpretation has become more flexible. Many countries are exploring alternatives to 
strict prohibition while remaining within, or strategically navigating, the international 
treaty framework. This has created a more pluralistic policy environment, in which 
experimentation and policy learning are more visible (Bewley-Taylor, 2016). 
The 2020 decision by the United Nations Commission on Narcotic Drugs to remove 
cannabis from Schedule IV of the 1961 Convention—while retaining it in Schedule I—
exemplifies this shift. It recognised the medical and therapeutic potential of cannabis 
without dismantling the broader prohibitionist framework. This development has 
created additional policy space for countries to pursue medical cannabis programmes 
within the international legal framework.
