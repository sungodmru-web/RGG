2.2 Unintended Consequences of Prohibition 
While prohibition has provided a coherent framework for cannabis control, its 
outcomes have been mixed and, in many cases, have generated unintended 
consequences that complicate policy objectives. These limitations are not uniform 
across contexts, but they are sufficiently widespread to have prompted a global 
reassessment of the approach (GCDP, 2018; UNODC, 2023). 
2.2.1 Limited Effectiveness 
Prohibition's failure is its limited effectiveness in reducing demand. Cannabis remains 
one of the most widely used controlled substances globally, with prevalence rates that 
have remained relatively stable or increased in several regions despite sustained 
enforcement efforts (Hall & Lynskey, 2016; UNODC, 2023). This persistence suggests 
that criminalisation alone is insufficient to deter use where social acceptance is evolving, 
and enforcement is inconsistent. 
Research on deterrence indicates that the severity of punishment has a relatively weak 
effect on cannabis use compared to other factors such as perceived health risks, social 
norms, and accessibility (Caulkins et al., 2012). This finding challenges a core 
assumption of prohibition: that criminal penalties effectively discourage consumption. 
While enforcement may influence behaviour at the margins, it does not appear to 
fundamentally alter aggregate patterns of use. 
On the supply side, prohibition has not eliminated production or distribution. Instead, 
it has contributed to the development of adaptive and resilient illicit markets. These 
markets operate outside formal regulatory systems, making them difficult to monitor 
and control. They can adjust rapidly to enforcement pressures, shifting production 
locations, distribution channels, and operational methods (Caulkins et al., 2012; Kilmer 
& Pacula, 2017). So, enforcement displaces rather than eliminates supply, creating a 
dynamic in which control efforts must continuously adapt to changing conditions. 
The "balloon effect7" is a well-documented phenomenon in drug control: pressure on 
supply in one location tends to displace production to other areas rather than reducing 
overall output. It has been observed in cannabis cultivation patterns globally, with 
production shifting across regions and countries in response to enforcement intensity.  
For LMIC, this dynamic can have significant implications, as displacement may drive 
production in areas with weaker governance and limited alternative livelihoods. The 
entrenchment of illicit cannabis within broader organised economic and informal trade 
systems presents a significant challenge to regulators.
