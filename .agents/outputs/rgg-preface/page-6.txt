The Book at a Glance  
In 2021, a smallholder farmer in Lesotho harvested what should have been a profitable 
cannabis crop. The plant was legal. The licence was valid. But without a functioning 
export certification system, a credible testing laboratory, or access to formal finance, the 
harvest went nowhere. The problem was never the cannabis — it was the governance. 
This book is about that gap: the space between legal reform and durable outcomes, 
between policy ambition and institutional reality. It is a gap that appears in every 
jurisdiction that has moved from prohibition toward regulated cannabis markets, and it 
is most consequential — and most neglected — in the countries this book is principally 
written for. 
Cannabis policy is undergoing a profound global transformation, shifting from 
prohibition toward a spectrum of regulatory models: medical access, decriminalisation, 
adult-use legalisation, and industrial hemp. This transition is neither linear nor uniform. 
Outcomes depend less on the formal legal status of cannabis than on the institutional 
capacity, policy coherence, and adaptive learning systems that states bring to 
implementation. Written primarily for policymakers, development practitioners, and 
governance reformers — those operating in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC) 
and Small Island Developing States (SIDS) — this book provides a comprehensive 
analytical framework for navigating that transition with clarity, realism, and strategic 
purpose. 
Core Analytical Framework 
The analytical framework of the book consists of these three interlocking principles:   
Differentiation recognises that countries operate under distinct historical, economic, 
and institutional conditions. Policy models developed in high-income jurisdictions 
cannot be transplanted to LMIC or SIDS without significant adaptation; design must 
be grounded in context-specific realities. What works in Canada will not automatically 
work in Cameroon. What succeeds in Uruguay requires fundamental rethinking before 
it can serve Jamaica or Mauritius. 
Institutional readiness holds that reform ambition must align with administrative 
capacity. Effective cannabis policy depends on the ability to license operators, monitor 
compliance, ensure product standards, and coordinate across sectors. Experience shows 
that phased, proportionate approaches succeed, while abrupt overhauls often 
overwhelm weaker systems. Ambition is not a substitute for capacity — and in this 
policy domain, the cost of that confusion falls hardest on the most vulnerable. 
Adaptive governance treats policy not as a fixed settlement enacted at a single 
moment, but as an evolving process of calibration, learning, and recalibration amid 
persistent uncertainty. No jurisdiction has fully solved cannabis governance. The ones 
succeeding are those building systems capable of honest self-assessment and continuous 
adaptation.
