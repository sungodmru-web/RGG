1.4.4 Sustainability Principles 
The transition from informal to formal systems raises equity and inclusion 
considerations. Small-scale producers risk exclusion if regulatory requirements are too 
stringent or capital-intensive. Policies must lower barriers to entry, support cooperative 
models, and recognise existing knowledge and practices. Failure to do so could produce 
a dual system of formal markets coexisting with persistent informality. 
Environmental sustainability is another critical factor. Cannabis cultivation can be 
resource-intensive if poorly managed, but it can also support sustainable agriculture 
when integrated into diversified cropping systems. Hemp offers potential benefits for 
soil health, carbon sequestration, and reduced reliance on synthetic inputs (UNEP, 
2011). Environmental alignment is essential for the viability of cannabis production. 
Ultimately, development pathways associated with cannabis in LMIC are neither 
automatic nor guaranteed. They depend on strategic policy choices, institutional 
capacity, and integration of cannabis into broader economic and environmental 
frameworks. Cannabis should not be treated as a stand-alone sector but as part of a 
wider transformation towards more diversified, resilient, and inclusive economies. 
Countries must be able to shape their own trajectories within a rapidly evolving global 
landscape — that is the governance challenge that cannot be deferred. 
1.5 Analytical Framework 
Cannabis reform is often approached as a legal or sector issue. This book adopts a 
different perspective, treating cannabis reform as a governance transition embedded 
within broader processes of economic transformation, institutional development, and 
policy adaptation. This shifts the focus from legal status to system performance: how 
policies are designed as living documents, implemented, and adjusted over time. Three 
interrelated principles underpin this analytical framework: differentiation, institutional 
readiness, and adaptive governance. Together, they offer a structured framework for 
understanding the diversity of cannabis policy pathways and the factors that determine 
their success or failure. 
1.5.1 Differentiation 
Differentiation recognises that countries operate under distinct historical, economic, 
and institutional conditions. Cannabis policy cannot be standardised across contexts. 
Factors such as income levels, governance capacity, agricultural structures, and social 
norms shape both the feasibility and desirability of policy options. Models developed in 
higher-income countries cannot be transferred directly to LMIC without significant 
adaptation. Policy design must be grounded in context-specific realities rather than 
imported templates (Rodrik, 2007). This principle reflects a common but differentiated 
approach to governance. While overarching goals—public health protection, equitable 
development, and effective regulation—may be widely shared, the pathways and 
institutional mechanisms for achieving them must vary according to national capabilities 
and constraints.
