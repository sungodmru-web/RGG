3.1.2 Decentralised Regulation  
Early cannabis use was governed through informal but structured systems of regulation. 
Knowledge of cultivation methods, preparation techniques, dosage, and context of use 
was dictated by social norms, cultural practices, and early medical traditions. These 
systems provided a form of decentralised control, ensuring that use remained aligned 
with social expectations and functional needs. Regulation, in this sense, was not absent; 
it was locally grounded and adaptive. The transmission of cannabis-related knowledge 
occurred primarily through oral traditions, apprenticeship systems, and practical 
experience rather than formal legal codes. Healers, shamans, and elders served as 
custodians of knowledge about appropriate preparation, dosing, and uses.  
This decentralised governance system allowed communities to adapt cultivation and use 
practices to local conditions while maintaining social accountability and customary 
oversight. From a governance viewpoint, this early phase offers critical insights. First, it 
demonstrates that cannabis has historically been managed through pluralistic systems 
rather than centralised legal frameworks. Second, it highlights the importance of 
context-specific knowledge in shaping effective regulation. Third, it underscores that 
the relationship between societies and cannabis has been dynamic, evolving in response 
to changing economic, cultural, and environmental conditions. 
The transition to prohibition in the twentieth century thus represents not the emergence 
of regulation, but a fundamental shift from decentralised, knowledge-based systems to 
centralised, law-based control. This shift disrupted long-standing practices and 
contributed to the disconnection between formal legal frameworks and lived realities 
that persists in many contexts today. Understanding the early domestication and use of 
cannabis is thus essential for contemporary policy. It reveals that governance is not a 
new challenge but an enduring feature of human interaction with the plant. It also 
suggests that effective regulation must reconcile formal systems with the historical and 
social foundations upon which cannabis use has long been embedded. 
3.2 India - The Ayurvedic System 
India represents one of the most refined historical integrations of cannabis within a 
structured knowledge system. Unlike many other regions where cannabis use remained 
diffuse or primarily utilitarian, the Indian subcontinent developed a coherent framework 
that combined medicinal, cultural, and regulatory dimensions. It was rooted in the Vedic 
and Ayurvedic traditions8, and fully integrated into the society.  
                                                   
8 The Vedic tradition refers to the ancient spiritual, philosophical and knowledge systems of the Indian subcontinent, rooted 
in the Vedas and later elaborated through practices such as Ayurveda. It highlights how plant-based knowledge was 
historically embedded within wider understandings of health, balance, ritual and wellbeing.
