2.6.3 SIDS: Niche Strategies  
In Small Island Developing States, cannabis policy is shaped by distinct constraints and 
opportunities. Limited land availability, high import dependence, and vulnerability to 
external shocks restrict the scope for large-scale agricultural production. At the same 
time, SIDS may be well-positioned to develop niche cannabis markets linked to 
wellness, tourism, and high-value, low-volume products. Tourism, a key sector in many 
SIDS economies, offers opportunities to align with cannabis policy. Wellness tourism 
may include cannabis-based therapies, spa treatments, and other curated experiences.  
However, this requires careful regulatory design to manage the interface between visitor 
access and domestic policy objectives, while avoiding enforcement challenges or social 
tensions linked to differential access. These niche opportunities align with broader 
strategies for economic diversification and sustainability, but they also require careful 
management to avoid excessive dependence on volatile external markets (UN-
OHRLLS, 2021). Diversification across several niches/segments, rather than reliance 
on a single product or market, can strengthen resilience. 
For SIDS, branding and differentiation are likely to matter more than scale. 
Emphasising distinctive attributes, such as unique cultivars, traditional cultivation 
practices, or sustainability credentials, can help position products in premium market 
segments. Certification schemes, geographical indications, and provenance-based 
storytelling can further support this positioning. 
2.6.4 Cross-Cutting Issues 
Institutional capacity remains a defining factor across LMIC. Effective cannabis 
governance requires coordination across sectors, including agriculture, health, trade, and 
finance. In many countries, however, these mechanisms remain underdeveloped and 
regulatory responsibilities fragmented. Strengthening inter-agency coordination, 
clarifying mandates, and investing in regulatory infrastructure are critical components 
of any policy pathway.  
The integration of informal systems is another key consideration. In many LMIC, 
informal cannabis production reflects deeper economic realities, including limited 
employment opportunities and restricted access to formal markets. Policies that ignore 
these dynamics risk reinforcing exclusion or perpetuating parallel systems. Simplified 
licensing, phased compliance, and targeted support can help formalise markets while 
protecting livelihoods. 
Environmental sustainability is also important to policy design. In regions highly 
exposed to climate variability, cannabis cultivation must align with sustainable land and 
resource management practices. Industrial hemp is attractive because of its relatively 
low environmental footprint and versatility across sectors. Therefore, integrating 
cannabis into broader green and blue economy strategies can strengthen resilience and 
long-term value creation. Financing and investment frameworks further shape policy 
pathways. Domestic producers and small enterprises often face limited access to capital, 
while foreign investors may possess greater financial resources and regulatory expertise.
