This section emphasises the concept of a governance continuum as a framework for 
understanding this diversity. Rather than classifying policies as either prohibitive or 
permissive, the continuum approach recognises a range of intermediate models, each 
with distinct institutional requirements, policy objectives, and risk profiles. 
2.4.1 Regulatory Pathways 
At one end of the continuum lies strict prohibition, characterised by comprehensive 
criminalisation and enforcement across the cannabis value chain. Under this model, 
production, distribution and use are subject to penal sanctions, with suppression as the 
primary policy objective. While this approach provides legal clarity, it relies heavily on 
enforcement capacity and offers limited scope to manage informal or illicit systems. 
Moving along the continuum, a second category may be described as de facto tolerance 
or selective enforcement. In these contexts, formal laws may remain prohibitive, but 
enforcement is uneven, discretionary or limited in scope. The result is a hybrid system 
in which informal markets operate alongside nominal legal restrictions. While this 
approach may reduce the immediate enforcement burden, it often lacks transparency 
and can weaken the legitimacy of policy frameworks. 
A third position is decriminalisation, where criminal penalties for possession or use are 
reduced or removed, typically replaced by administrative sanctions or no penalty at all. 
Decriminalisation shifts the focus away from the punitive control of users, often to 
reduce social harms associated with criminalisation. However, it does not address 
supply-side dynamics, which remain within the illicit domain. 
Further along the continuum are medical or controlled access frameworks. These 
systems allow for the regulated use of cannabis for therapeutic purposes under specific 
conditions, including prescription requirements and controlled distribution channels. 
They introduce elements of regulation and quality control, but remain limited in scope 
and are often tightly managed by state or authorised entities. 
At the more permissive end of the continuum are regulated market systems, where the 
production, distribution, and sale of cannabis are legal under a structured regulatory 
framework. These systems typically involve licensing, quality standards, taxation, and 
oversight mechanisms (Kilmer & Pacula, 2017). Their objectives extend beyond control 
to include public health, market regulation, and economic development. However, they 
also require significant institutional capacity to design and enforce complex regulatory 
systems. 
2.4.2 Hybrid Models  
Between and within these broad categories, numerous hybrid models exist. Countries 
may combine elements of different approaches, reflecting their specific policy priorities 
and institutional constraints. For example, a country may implement decriminalisation 
while simultaneously developing pilot programmes for medical use, or it may legalise 
certain aspects of the market while maintaining restrictions on others. The governance 
continuum highlights that cannabis policy is not static. Countries can move along the 
spectrum over time, adjusting their approaches as conditions evolve.
