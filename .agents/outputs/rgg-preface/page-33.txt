2.3 Drivers of Policy Transition 
The transition from prohibition to more governance-oriented approaches in cannabis 
policy is not the result of a single factor. It reflects the convergence of multiple drivers 
that, taken together, have altered both the feasibility and desirability of traditional 
control frameworks. These drivers operate across scientific, economic, social, and 
institutional domains, creating sustained pressure for policy adaptation. 
2.3.1 Scientific and Knowledge Drivers 
A primary driver of change is the evolution of scientific knowledge. Advances in 
research have expanded understanding of cannabis, regarding the Endocannabinoid 
system and the differentiated effects of cannabinoids. The discovery of cannabinoid 
receptors and endogenous cannabinoids in the 1990s fundamentally altered scientific 
understanding of how cannabis interacts with human physiology. This has led to a more 
nuanced assessment of both risks and potential therapeutic applications, challenging 
earlier classifications that treated cannabis as a uniformly harmful substance (NASEM, 
2017; WHO, 2019). 
The growing recognition of medical uses has created pressure for regulatory frameworks 
that allow controlled access while maintaining safeguards. Clinical research, while still 
constrained by regulatory barriers, has demonstrated efficacy for cannabis-based 
medicines in treating certain conditions, including chronic pain, chemotherapy-induced 
nausea, and spasticity associated with multiple sclerosis. This evidence base, while not 
definitive, has shifted the terms of policy debate. 
Concurrently, scientific uncertainty persists. Evidence on long-term health effects, 
dosage, and interactions with other substances is still evolving. This creates a complex 
policy environment in which decisions must often be made in the absence of complete 
information. Consequently, regulatory systems must be capable of applying existing 
knowledge and of adapting as new evidence emerges. 
2.3.2 Economic and Market Forces 
Economic dynamics represent a second major driver. The emergence of legal cannabis 
markets in several countries has demonstrated the scale of potential economic activity 
associated with the sector. These markets generate employment, investment, and fiscal 
revenues, altering the cost-benefit calculus of prohibition (Kilmer & Pacula, 2017). In 
countries that have legalised cannabis for adult use, such as Canada and several U.S. 
states, the economic impacts have been significant. Legal markets have created jobs in 
cultivation, processing, distribution, and retail.  
Ancillary industries, including testing laboratories, security services, legal and consulting 
firms, and technology providers, have also expanded. Tax revenues from cannabis sales 
have provided new fiscal resources, though their magnitude varies by tax structure and 
market maturity. Meanwhile, industrial hemp has gained attention as a versatile crop 
with applications in textiles, construction, and bio-based industries, reinforcing its 
relevance within green and circular economy strategies (FAO, 2022).
