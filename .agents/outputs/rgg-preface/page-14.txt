1.1 Cannabis at a Global Crossroads 
Cannabis occupies an increasingly prominent place in contemporary policy debates. 
Once confined to criminal justice, it has evolved into a complex domain intersecting 
with health systems, economic development, environmental sustainability, and social 
equity. This transformation reflects both changing societal attitudes and a growing 
recognition that cannabis policy carries implications far beyond its traditional framing. 
1.1.1 Competing Policy Paradigms 
Across regions, governments are adopting divergent approaches. Some maintain strict 
prohibition, prioritising deterrence and criminal penalties. Others have moved towards 
decriminalisation, seeking to reduce the social and economic costs of criminal 
enforcement. A smaller but growing number have introduced regulated systems 
governing production, distribution, and use. These systems vary widely in design, 
reflecting differences in policy objectives, institutional capacity, and socio-economic 
context (UNODC, 2023). 
This diversity underscores the absence of a universal policy model. Cannabis reform is 
characterised by experimentation and adaptation rather than a single trajectory. 
Countries learn from one another but also respond to domestic conditions shaping both 
feasibility and desirability (Bewley-Taylor et al., 2016). At the global level, this has 
produced a fragmented policy landscape in which national policies are increasingly 
shaped by internal dynamics rather than by uniform international norms. 
1.1.2 A Development and Governance Lens 
Cannabis's growing prominence is also linked to its expanding economic relevance. 
Legal markets for medicinal cannabis and industrial hemp are developing rapidly, 
generating new opportunities for investment, employment, and value creation. These 
developments reposition cannabis as a potential component of broader economic and 
industrial strategies. However, they also introduce new challenges, including market 
regulation, supply chain management, and the need to ensure broad-based participation 
(Kilmer & Pacula, 2017). 
Critically, the expansion of legal markets does not automatically displace informal or 
illicit systems. In many low- and middle-income countries (LMIC), informal markets 
remain dominant due to limited regulatory capacity, high compliance costs, and 
entrenched socio-economic practices. Cannabis policy must address both formal market 
development and the integration of existing informal systems. 
For LMIC, cannabis offers opportunities for agricultural diversification, rural 
livelihoods, and participation in emerging green industries. Industrial hemp, in 
particular, aligns with the objectives of sustainable production and climate resilience. 
However, these opportunities carry risks: unequal market access, regulatory capture, and 
exposure to better-resourced external investors. The notion of a "crossroads" captures 
both opportunity and uncertainty. Countries navigate complex choices under 
incomplete information and evolving evidence. Decisions taken now will have long-
term implications for economic structures, public health outcomes, and social equity.
