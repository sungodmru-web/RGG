1.4.2 Structural Asymmetries 
There is a risk that emerging cannabis markets will reproduce patterns observed in other 
commodity sectors: primary production in LMIC, while value addition concentrates 
elsewhere. Generally, domestic producers capture only a limited share of total value, 
constraining broader development gains (Rodrik, 2007; Gereffi, 2018). Therefore, 
informed strategies to promote local processing, technological upgrading, and 
integration into higher-value segments of the value chain are required.  
Structural weaknesses further complicate participation. LMIC often face limited access 
to finance, technology, and regulatory expertise, while international firms from 
developed markets navigate licensing systems and compliance requirements more easily 
(WB, 2020). Without appropriate safeguards, this can lead to market capture and 
marginalisation of smallholders, cooperatives, and domestic enterprises  
For the African continent, the strategic question is how to position the cannabis 
economy in national development frameworks. This includes decisions on land use, 
licensing regimes, export orientation, and the balance between domestic and foreign 
investment. It also involves aligning cannabis policy with broader objectives such as 
rural development, employment generation, and industrial diversification. Industrial 
hemp presents particular opportunities due to lower regulatory barriers and a wider 
range of industrial applications.  With appropriate investment, it can stimulate local 
manufacturing, encourage innovation, and strengthen regional value chains. A 
coordinated strategy can help maximise economic benefits while supporting 
environmental sustainability and social inclusion. (FAO, 2022). 
1.4.3 SIDS Context  
In Small Island Developing States, the context is distinct but equally complex. Limited 
land availability, high import dependence, and exposure to external shocks constrain 
economic options. Cannabis may offer niche opportunities in high-value markets, 
sustainability branding, and integration with the tourism or wellness sectors. But these 
opportunities require careful management to avoid over-dependence on volatile 
markets or externally driven regulatory frameworks (UN-OHRLLS, 2021). Institutional 
capacity is decisive across all contexts. Effective participation in regulated cannabis 
markets requires systems for licensing, quality control, inspection, and data management 
(Acemoglu & Robinson, 2012). It also requires coordination across agriculture, health, 
trade, and finance. In most LMIC, these systems are still evolving, making phased and 
adaptive approaches essential.
