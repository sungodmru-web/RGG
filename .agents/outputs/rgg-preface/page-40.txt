2.5.4 Regulated Commercial Systems 
At the more market-oriented end of the spectrum are regulated commercial systems. 
These frameworks legalise production, distribution, and sale under a licensing regime 
that includes quality standards, taxation, and compliance requirements. They aim to shift 
cannabis activity from illicit to regulated markets while generating economic and fiscal 
benefits. Canada's Cannabis Act (2018) established a regulated commercial system 
combining private-sector participation with federal and provincial oversight. 
The model demonstrates the potential for economic development and fiscal revenue 
generation, while also highlighting challenges related to market concentration and 
regulatory complexity. However, these systems are complex to design and implement. 
They require robust regulatory institutions, effective monitoring systems, and 
mechanisms to prevent excessive market capture or commercialisation that may conflict 
with public health objectives (Caulkins et al., 2016). Balancing commercial viability with 
public health protection remains an ongoing challenge. 
2.5.5 Hybrid Models  
Between these categories, a range of hybrid models has emerged. Countries may 
combine decriminalisation with medical access, introduce pilot programmes for 
regulated supply, or maintain state control over certain segments while allowing private 
participation in others. These hybrid approaches reflect efforts to balance competing 
objectives, such as public health, economic development, and social equity. An 
important dimension across all models is the treatment of informal systems.  
In many LMIC, existing cannabis production is embedded in informal economies. 
Regulatory models that fail to reflect this reality risk excluding large groups of the 
population. They may also reinforce dual systems in which formal and informal markets 
coexist. Hence, inclusive design, through mechanisms such as simplified licensing, 
cooperative models, or phased formalisation, is critical. 
2.5.6 Strategic Choices 
Another key consideration is regulatory complexity. Models that are highly effective in 
high-capacity environments may not be directly transferable to contexts with limited 
institutional resources. Overly complex systems can create barriers to entry, increase 
compliance costs, and inadvertently sustain informal markets. This underscores the 
need to align regulatory design with institutional readiness, as outlined in Chapter 1.  
Fiscal and economic objectives also vary across models. Some frameworks prioritise 
revenue generation through taxation and licensing, while others emphasise public health 
or social objectives. Balancing these priorities is critical, as excessive taxation or 
regulatory burden can sustain illicit markets, while insufficient control can undermine 
health and safety outcomes. For LMIC, the choice of regulatory model is both a 
technical and a strategic decision. It involves assessing trade-offs between control, 
inclusiveness, economic potential, and institutional ability. Phased and adaptive 
approaches are often more effective than complex systems from the outset.
