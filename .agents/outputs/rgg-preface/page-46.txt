2.7.3 Policy Implications 
This shift has important implications for policymaking. It requires moving beyond static 
regulatory models towards adaptive systems that respond to evolving evidence, market 
dynamics, public health priorities, and societal expectations. It also demands greater 
policy coherence, as cannabis governance spans public health, agriculture, trade, finance, 
justice, and environmental management. Fragmented approaches often create 
regulatory gaps, weaken implementation, and reduce policy effectiveness. For LMIC, 
this governance paradigm is particularly relevant. Institutional capacity constraints, high 
levels of informality, and competing development priorities shape both the pace and 
feasibility of reform. The challenge is not to replicate externally developed models, but 
to design context-specific approaches that reflect national realities while remaining 
responsive to international obligations and evolving global markets. Incremental 
reforms, supported by institutional learning and stakeholder engagement, can help build 
more resilient and effective governance systems.

small-scale producers, local enterprises, and historically marginalised communities risk 
being excluded from emerging opportunities. Inclusive participation is therefore both a 
social imperative and a prerequisite for building legitimate, competitive, and sustainable 
cannabis economies. Environmental sustainability adds another critical dimension. 
Poorly managed cannabis production can increase pressure on land, water, energy, and 
biodiversity. Conversely, when integrated into sustainable agricultural systems, 
particularly through industrial hemp, cannabis can support soil regeneration, promote 
low-carbon materials, encourage circular bioeconomy approaches, and contribute to 
more diversified and climate-resilient production systems.  
2.7.5 Testing Governance Capacity 
The shift towards a governance paradigm reflects a broader transformation in public 
policy. Complex challenges cannot be addressed through single-dimensional 
instruments or isolated institutions. They require governance systems capable of 
balancing competing objectives, managing trade-offs, coordinating across sectors, and 
adapting to changing circumstances. Cannabis illustrates this complexity because it 
intersects with public health, justice, agriculture, trade, finance, and social equity, 
demanding integrated rather than siloed responses. Cannabis policy is therefore best 
understood not simply as a question of legal status, but as a test of governance capacity. 
The objective is not to eliminate complexity, but to manage it through institutions that 
are effective, resilient, inclusive, and adaptable. Countries that build these governance 
capabilities are likely to be better positioned to respond to future challenges while 
delivering more balanced and sustainable policy outcomes.
