1.3 Convergence of Science and Governance 
The convergence of three interrelated forces drives the transformation of cannabis 
policy in the twenty-first century: advances in scientific knowledge, rapid economic 
expansion, and growing governance complexity. These forces interact to reshape both 
the understanding of cannabis and the policy frameworks designed to manage it. 
1.3.1 Scientific Understanding 
Science has been the catalyst. Research on the endocannabinoid system and diverse 
cannabinoids has fundamentally reframed cannabis — from a uniform substance of 
abuse to a compound with a complex profile of properties, risks, and potential 
applications (NASEM, 2017). Recognition of therapeutic uses for certain cannabis-
derived products has challenged earlier assumptions and opened new areas of clinical 
and regulatory interest. Nonetheless, scientific uncertainty persists. Evidence on long-
term health effects, dosage, and interactions remains incomplete. Regulatory systems 
must remain evidence-based and adaptive. 
1.3.2 Economic Relevance 
Parallel to scientific developments, cannabis has become an increasingly significant 
economic sector. Legal markets for medicinal cannabis and industrial hemp are 
expanding across multiple regions, generating investment, employment, and value 
creation. These markets encompass activities from cultivation and processing to 
pharmaceuticals, textiles, and bio-based materials. This economic expansion reshapes 
perceptions of cannabis. It is no longer seen solely as a regulatory burden but also as a 
potential driver of economic development. For countries seeking economic 
diversification or new export sectors, cannabis represents an emerging opportunity. 
However, these prospects are unevenly distributed and carry risks such as market 
concentration, volatility, and barriers for smaller producers (Kilmer & Pacula, 2017). 
1.3.3 Institutional Coordination 
The intersection of science and markets creates new governance demands. Regulatory 
frameworks must address traditional concerns of public health and safety alongside 
issues of market structure, competition, and equity. It requires institutional coordination 
and technical capacity, which are often difficult to achieve, notably in LMIC. 
Governance, in this context, extends beyond the enactment of law. It encompasses the 
full range of mechanisms through which policies are designed, implemented, monitored, 
and revised. Effective systems must integrate multiple objectives, including risk 
management, economic development, and social inclusion. They require coordination 
across ministries responsible for health, agriculture, trade, finance, and justice.
