1.4 Cannabis Development 
While early cannabis reforms have concentrated in higher-income countries, the 
transition is increasingly significant for LMIC. Contextually, cannabis is not merely a 
regulatory issue but a potential lever for structural transformation—intersecting 
agriculture, rural livelihoods, industrial policy, and global value chain3 participation. 
When supported by sound governance, it can also stimulate innovation, value addition, 
and local enterprise. Realising these benefits depends on coherent policies, capable 
institutions, and equitable market access (WB, 2017; UNDP, 2015). 
1.4.1 Emerging Markets  
In many LMIC, particularly across the African continent, agriculture is the primary 
source of employment and income, with smallholder systems dominating. Cannabis, 
especially industrial hemp, offers opportunities for crop diversification, value addition, 
and integration into bio-based industries. Its applications span fibres, textiles, 
construction materials, nutraceuticals, and pharmaceuticals, supporting sustainable 
production and circular economy strategies (UNEP, 2011; FAO, 2022). 
Yet cannabis has long been part of the agricultural and cultural landscape in many of 
these economies. It has long been cultivated informally4 in remote and marginal areas 
outside formal regulatory systems. These informal value chains provide livelihoods in 
contexts where alternatives are limited and operate with low productivity, limited quality 
control, and high exposure to enforcement (UNODC, 2023).
