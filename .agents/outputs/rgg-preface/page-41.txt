2.5.7 Illustrative National Trajectories 
The diversity of regulatory models is reflected in the varied trajectories adopted by 
different countries. While contexts differ, these examples help to ground the analysis.  
Uruguay represents a state-controlled model in which the government regulates 
production, distribution, and retail. This approach prioritises public control and limits 
commercialisation, but requires strong administrative capacity and sustained state 
involvement. 
Canada has adopted a regulated commercial system that combines private-sector 
participation with federal and provincial oversight. The model demonstrates the 
potential for economic development and fiscal revenue generation, while also 
highlighting challenges related to market concentration and regulatory complexity. 
Morocco has recently moved towards a development-oriented framework, legalising 
cannabis cultivation for medical and industrial purposes within a regulated system aimed 
at integrating small farmers. This approach reflects an effort to shift from informal 
production to formal value chains. 
Lesotho has positioned itself as an export-oriented producer of medical cannabis. 
While this strategy has attracted foreign investment, it has also raised questions 
regarding local participation, value addition, and long-term sustainability. 
These examples show that policy pathways are shaped by national priorities, institutional 
capacity, and economic strategy. They also reinforce the importance of aligning 
regulatory models with broader development objectives. 
Ultimately, no single model can be considered universally optimal. Each approach 
reflects a different configuration of objectives, constraints, and institutional capacities. 
The relevance of any model depends on how well it aligns with national priorities and 
how effectively it can be implemented and adjusted over time. 
2.6 Policy Pathways for LMIC  
The transition from prohibition to governance-oriented cannabis policy presents a set 
of strategic choices for these groups. These choices reflect institutional capacity, 
economic priorities, and the scale of informal systems. The proliferation of regulatory 
models is only half the story. How countries navigate the path from policy choice to 
institutional reality is equally important — and often more so. 
2.6.1 Sequencing Reform with Institutional Readiness 
For LMIC, the challenge is not only selecting an appropriate regulatory model but also 
sequencing reforms to match institutional readiness. Adopting complex systems, 
particularly those modelled on high-income countries, can exceed administrative 
capacity and create implementation gaps. Phased approaches, beginning with measures 
such as decriminalisation or pilot programmes, offer a more practical entry point. They 
allow governments to build regulatory capacity, strengthen institutions, and improve 
coordination over time. (WB, 2017).
